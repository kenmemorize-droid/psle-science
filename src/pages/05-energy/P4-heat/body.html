Heat in five ideas

1 Heat is energy, and where it comes from

Heat is a form of energy. It cannot be seen, but it can be felt. A source of heat is anything that produces heat:

  • the Sun
  • burning things, such as a candle, wood or fuel
  • switched-on electrical appliances, such as an iron, a toaster or an oven
  • rubbing two things together

Most sources of light are also sources of heat. Ice is not a source of "cold": cold is just less heat.

2 Temperature, and how it differs from heat

Temperature is a measurement of how hot something is: its degree of hotness. It is measured in degrees Celsius (°C) with a thermometer, or with a datalogger and a temperature sensor, which records the temperature again and again over time.

Heat is not the same as temperature. Two beakers of water are both at 60 °C, but beaker B holds twice as much water as beaker A. They have the same temperature, yet B has more heat, because more heat was needed to warm more water to 60 °C. With the same amount of water, the hotter one has more heat.

3 Heat flows from hotter to colder

Heat always flows from a hotter object or place to a colder one, until both reach the same temperature. Then it stops.

  • An object that gains heat rises in temperature.
  • An object that loses heat falls in temperature.

A drink with ice in it gets colder because the drink loses heat to the ice. No "cold" flows out of the ice.

4 Effects of heat gain and loss
  • Expansion and contraction. Solids, liquids and gases expand (increase in volume) when they gain heat, and contract (decrease in volume) when they lose heat. Their mass stays the same.
  • Everyday uses: gaps between railway tracks and concrete slabs; a movable joint at one end of a bridge; overhead cables hung loosely; bottles and cans not filled to the top; heating a metal tyre so it expands, fitting it on a wheel, then letting it cool.
  • Change of state. Gaining heat can make ice melt and water boil or evaporate. Losing heat can make water freeze and water vapour condense.
5 Good and poor conductors of heat
  • A good conductor lets heat flow through it easily. Metals are good conductors.
  • A poor conductor does not. Wood, plastics, air and rubber are poor conductors.
  • Cooking pots are metal, so heat reaches the food quickly. Pot handles are plastic or wood, so the hand stays safe.
  • A metal bench feels colder than a wooden one at the same temperature, because the metal conducts heat away from your hand faster.

Everyday uses (tap each one)

Railway tracks

Small gaps between sections give the tracks room to expand on hot days, so they do not bend.

Concrete pavement

Gaps give the concrete room to expand and contract as the temperature changes, so it does not crack.

Pipes

Pipes carrying hot liquids have curved joints or loops, which give room to expand and contract so the pipes do not break.

Bridges

One end is fixed and the other has a movable joint, so the bridge can expand on hot days and contract on cold days.

Overhead cables

Cables are hung loosely so they can contract on cold days without snapping, but not so loosely that they sag dangerously when they expand on hot days.

Bottles and cans

They are not filled to the top, leaving room for the drink to expand so the container does not burst.

Wheel and tyre

A metal tyre is heated so it expands, slipped over the wheel, then left to cool. It contracts and grips the wheel tightly.

Beyond syllabus Surface area, colour and texture

Two more things change how fast heat is transferred. They are not in the P4 syllabus and are never tested here.

  • Surface area. The larger the surface touching a heat source or the surroundings, the faster heat is transferred. A pot with a wide base boils sooner on a hot plate. An African elephant's large ears help it lose heat.
  • Colour and texture. A black, dull, rough surface absorbs heat faster than a white, shiny, smooth one. Alice left two tins in the sun:
