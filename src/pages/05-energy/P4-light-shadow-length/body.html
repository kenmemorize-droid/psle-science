Key idea. A shadow always points away from its own lamp. With a lamp above you shining down on the ground, the farther you are from the lamp, the more the light slants, and the longer your shadow. Right under the lamp, your shadow is only a small patch at your feet.
Why is this different from the torch and wall test? In class, moving an object closer to the torch makes its shadow on the wall bigger, because the wall stands behind the object. Here the shadow lies on the ground and the lamp is above the person. Both follow the same rule: light travels in straight lines, and the shadow is wherever the object stops it.
Beyond syllabus A rule that predicts the shadow

The light ray, the person and the ground make two triangles with the same shape. This gives:

shadow = person × distance ÷ (lamp − person)

If the lamp were not higher than the person's head, no shadow would reach the ground.

Beyond syllabus Raise the lamps

Raise both lamps with the slider. Both shadows get shorter: a higher lamp sends its light down more steeply, so the person blocks less ground.

Beyond syllabus Two lamps, so two shadows

Where only one lamp is blocked, the other lamp still lights the ground, so that part is lighter. Where both are blocked, the ground is darkest. Switch to the 3D view and the top-down camera to see this.

Challenge: drag the person until the two shadows are exactly equal. How far is the person from each lamp?
