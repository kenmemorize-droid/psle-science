The two rules that decide everything

Rule 1. A compass needle is a small magnet that can turn freely. The shaded (coloured) end is its N pole, drawn in the same colour as a magnet's N pole. With no magnet nearby, it comes to rest pointing North–South, like any freely suspended magnet.
Rule 2. Unlike poles attract and like poles repel. So the needle's N end is pulled towards a S pole and pushed away from a N pole.

Bring a magnet close and the magnet decides where the needle points. It does not need to touch the compass.

   

Drag the magnet far away from the big compass. The needle swings back to point north: with no magnet close by, a freely suspended magnet rests pointing North–South.

Beyond syllabus Why "north" at all? The Earth as a giant magnet

The Earth itself behaves like one enormous magnet. That is why a freely suspended magnet, left alone, comes to rest pointing North–South, and why the shaded end of a compass needle is called the north-seeking end.

Tap a compass on the Earth.

If the needle's N end is attracted to the Earth's magnet, then the end of the Earth's magnet near the geographic North must be a magnetic S pole, because unlike poles attract. It is still called "the north magnetic pole" because of where it is on the map, which is where the confusion comes from. The Earth's magnetic poles also drift a little every year.

None of this is tested at PSLE. For PSLE you need only this: a freely suspended magnet comes to rest pointing North–South, and a magnet nearby changes where it points. You never need the words "magnetic field" or "magnetic induction" in an answer.
