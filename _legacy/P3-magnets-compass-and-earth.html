Why a compass needle swings — magnets, poles and the Earth

A compass needle is just a tiny magnet that is free to turn. Everything it does follows two rules you already know.

Syllabus learning outcomes targeted
  • Recognise that a magnet can exert a push or a pull.
  • Magnets have two poles. A freely suspended bar magnet comes to rest pointing in a North–South direction.
  • Unlike poles attract and like poles repel.
  • Magnets attract magnetic materials.

1. The two rules that decide everything

Rule 1. A compass needle is a small magnet that can turn freely. The shaded (dark) end is its N pole.
Rule 2. Unlike poles attract, like poles repel. So the needle’s N end is pulled towards a S pole and pushed away from a N pole.

With no magnet nearby, the needle settles pointing North–South. Bring a magnet close and the magnet wins — its push and pull are far stronger than the Earth’s.

   
Move the magnet far from the big compass. The needle swings back to North, because now only the Earth is acting on it.

2. Why “north” at all?

The Earth itself behaves like one enormous magnet. That is why a freely suspended magnet, left alone, always comes to rest pointing in a North–South direction — and why the shaded end of a compass needle is called the north-seeking end.

For PSLE you only need this: a freely suspended magnet or compass comes to rest pointing in a North–South direction, and a nearby magnet overrides that.
Beyond syllabus The part that trips people up

If the Earth is a giant bar magnet, and the needle’s N end is attracted to it… then the end of the Earth’s magnet buried near the geographic North must be a magnetic S pole. Unlike poles attract — so N seeks S.

Scientists still call it “the north magnetic pole” by its position on the map, which is where the confusion comes from. The Earth’s magnetic poles also drift a little every year, and they are not exactly on the geographic poles.

None of this is tested at PSLE, and you never need the words “magnetic field” or “magnetic induction” in an answer. It is here only so the picture makes sense.
